Which road was the car really on?—

GPS points drift off the road: onto the next street, across buildings, or into gaps. For every GPS point we pick the road segment the vehicle was really on. A machine-learning classifier scores the nearby roads, and an HMM with Viterbi decoding joins those scores into one connected route.

Pipeline

From noisy GPS to a connected route

1

Clean and project. Read the GPS track, nodes and arcs. Drop duplicate timestamps and single-point jumps faster than 180 km/h. Convert longitude and latitude to metres.

Why: one degree of longitude here is about 75 km, but one degree of latitude is about 111 km, so distances in degrees would be wrong.

2

Candidate roads. A KD-tree finds every arc within 50 m of each GPS point and keeps the nearest 10.

Why: it turns "which of thousands of roads?" into a small choice. The true road is in the list for more than 99% of points.

3

Features and labels. Distance, distance rank, gap to the nearest road, heading difference, speed, arc length, position along the arc, number of candidates, time gap.

Why: the right road is usually close and points the way the car is moving. Labels: the nearest route arc within 30 m. Points the route doesn't cover are left out.

4

Classifier. Gives each candidate a probability of being the true road. Trained on 80 trips with grouped cross-validation, then tuned with Bayesian optimisation (Optuna TPE).

Why: the HMM needs probabilities, so we also track log loss, not just accuracy.

5

HMM + Viterbi. Hidden states are the candidate roads. Emission is the classifier probability. Transition is high when two roads connect, a small cost per extra arc, and a big penalty for unconnected jumps. Viterbi finds the best chain.

Why: scored one point at a time, the classifier can jump between unconnected roads. The HMM uses the whole sequence to keep the route continuous. After a GPS gap of more than 10 s, any road change is allowed.

Dataset · from results/eda and results/features

What the data looks like

Each trip has a GPS track (lon, lat, time, about 1 per second), road nodes, road arcs (from node → to node) and a reference route (an ordered list of arc ids).

Key finding: the reference route doesn't always cover the whole track

In some training trips the route covers only part of the GPS track. In trip 54 only about 12% of points are within 30 m of the route. We label and score only the points that have a reference road nearby, and report this coverage openly.
